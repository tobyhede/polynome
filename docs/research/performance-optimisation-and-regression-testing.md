# Performance optimisation and regression testing for Polynome

> **Scope:** what actually governs perceived performance in a browser metronome of this shape, which optimisations would be measurable rather than theatrical, and what a performance regression suite can assert without flaking on a shared CI runner. External claims cite the W3C Web Audio API specification, the WHATWG HTML standard, MDN, Chrome-team documentation on web.dev and developer.chrome.com, Chromium source, Node.js and Playwright documentation. Numbers attributed to this repository were measured against the working tree at `c2d1914` on an Apple M2 under Node 22.23.1; the method and its limits are recorded at the end, and everything that could not be verified is listed there too.

## Conclusion

**The audio scheduler is not the cost centre, and no amount of optimising it will be visible.** The whole main-thread scheduler tick — `#syncNodes()`, `plan()`, and committing every click — costs **under 0.004 ms at the default configuration and 0.017 ms at the domain maximum of twelve 8/4 layers at subdivision four running at 300 BPM**. The interval it has to fit inside is 25 ms. That is 0.068% of the budget in the worst case the domain permits. `plan()` alone, the inner loop the whole design is arranged around, is **0.00010 ms per tick at the default** and **0.00721 ms at the maximum**. Nothing in `shared-transport.js` or `model.js` is worth optimising for time.

**What the measurements do find is traffic, not time.** At the default single 4/4 layer the engine issues **126 AudioParam automation calls per second, of which 120 are `setTargetAtTime` calls that change nothing** — `#syncNodes()` re-asserts the master gain, every layer gain and every pan on every one of the forty ticks per second, whether or not the mix moved. At twelve layers it is 1,000 such calls per second out of 1,720. Ninety-five per cent of the default configuration's automation traffic is a no-op restating values already in force. This is the one finding that is both large as a ratio and cheap to fix.

**The second finding is garbage, on the thread that must not be interrupted.** A Peak Cycle BPM envelope makes `secondsAtBeat` and `beatAtSeconds` rebuild their two sub-curves through `createTempoCurve` on every single call. One `beatAtSeconds` on a Peak curve costs **302.7 ns against 20.1 ns on a Flat one, a factor of 15**, and allocates four frozen objects. `plan()` alone allocates **3,239 of them per second** at the domain maximum, before the per-click `currentBpm()` call adds roughly 960 more. The time is irrelevant; the allocation is not, because [Chris Wilson names garbage collection as one of the things that skews the very timer this design depends on](https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling).

**The look-ahead scheduler is correct as written and slightly more conservative than the canonical recipe.** The Chrome-team article the pattern comes from says *"A good place to start is probably 100ms of 'lookahead' time, with intervals set to 25ms"* ([A Tale of Two Clocks](https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling)). Polynome runs 25 ms and 0.12 s, leaving 95 ms of overlap — nearly four tick intervals of slack against the three the article suggests. Nothing here needs changing.

**Per-click `OscillatorNode` + `GainNode` allocation is the design the specification prescribes, not a cost to engineer around.** The spec states outright that *"The Web Audio API takes a fire-and-forget approach to audio source scheduling. That is, source nodes are created for each note during the lifetime of the AudioContext, and never explicitly removed from the graph"* ([§1.1.6](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#lack-of-introspection-or-serialization-primitives)). What the spec calls expensive is the *context*, not the nodes: *"The subclasses AudioContext and OfflineAudioContext should be considered expensive objects"* ([§1.1.7](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#system-resources-associated-with-baseaudiocontext-subclasses)). MDN says of the equivalent buffer source that *"these nodes are very inexpensive to create"* ([AudioBufferSourceNode](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioBufferSourceNode)). Node pooling and an `AudioWorklet` are both unwarranted here.

**Rendering is where the milliseconds are, and the repo already has the only measurement of it.** [ADR-0009](../adr/0009-adopt-preact-as-the-renderer.md) recorded a step click costing 1.992 ms to reconcile at 384 step controls, against 7.496 ms for the `innerHTML` rebuild it replaced. That is three orders of magnitude above anything the transport does, and 12% of a 16.7 ms frame. The per-animation-frame playhead loop is the other half, and it was not measurable from Node: its transport arithmetic is **0.011 ms per frame at the domain maximum**, but it also issues **39 selector-engine calls and 13 `CSS.escape` calls per frame** — 2,340 and 780 per second at 60 Hz — none of which was measured in a browser here.

**For regression testing, count work, do not time it.** Over 200 identical runs of the worst-case planning workload, `plan()` returned exactly one distinct event count. Over 60 timed runs of the same workload on an idle laptop, wall time varied by **8.7% coefficient of variation and a 1.47× max/min spread** — and normalising against a control workload run in the same process *did not help*, coming out at 8.2% and 1.69×. Naive heap-delta measurement was worse than useless: across four runs the Flat configuration measured 481–954 KiB and the Peak configuration 125–787 KiB, with the ordering inverting between runs. The deterministic assertions this repository can make — allocated node counts through the existing `options.createContext` seam, planned-event counts and ordering, DOM operation counts through instrumented stubs, and artifact byte size — are exact, free, and catch the regressions that actually matter.

## 1. What governs perceived performance in an app of this shape

Four budgets, on three different clocks, and only one of them is the audio thread's.

| Budget | Clock | Size | What blows it | Polynome's measured position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Audio render | `AudioContext.currentTime`, advancing 128 frames at a time | one render quantum, 2.67 ms at 48 kHz | render-thread overload, buffer underrun | not reachable from Node; nothing in the graph is per-sample JavaScript |
| Scheduler jitter | `setInterval`, main thread | 25 ms tick, 120 ms horizon, 95 ms of slack | main-thread stalls: layout, GC, reconciliation | worst measured tick 0.017 ms; worst measured render stall 1.992 ms (ADR-0009) |
| Frame | rendering opportunities | ~16.7 ms at 60 Hz | layout thrash, reconciliation, per-frame DOM queries | 1.992 ms reconcile; playhead DOM cost unmeasured |
| Load | wall clock, once | no stated budget | bundle bytes, parse | 257,726 bytes single-file, 151,364 bytes of script in the site build |

### (a) The audio thread

The rendering thread processes audio in blocks: *"The number of sample-frames in a block is called render quantum size, and the block itself is called a render quantum. Its default value is 128"* ([§ Rendering an audio graph](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#render-quantum)). `currentTime` *"is updated by the rendering thread in uniform increments, corresponding to one render quantum"* ([§1.1.1](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dom-baseaudiocontext-currenttime)), which is why it is the only legitimate basis for a musical timestamp and why AGENTS.md forbids deriving one from `setInterval`.

Two latency figures are specified and they mean different things. `baseLatency` *"represents the number of seconds of processing latency incurred by the AudioContext passing the audio from the AudioDestinationNode to the audio subsystem"*, with the spec's worked example giving *"(2·128)/44100 = 5.805 ms"* for a double-buffered implementation. `outputLatency` is *"the estimation in seconds of audio output latency, i.e., the interval between the time the UA requests the host system to play a buffer and the time at which the first sample in the buffer is actually processed by the audio output device"*, and the spec warns that it *"may change while the context is running or the associated audio output device changes"* and that *"it is useful to query this value frequently when accurate synchronization is required"* ([§1.2.2](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dom-audiocontext-baselatency)). Neither affects Polynome's internal correctness — every click is placed on the same clock, so a constant output offset shifts the whole grid — but both bound how tightly a visual playhead can be aligned to what a listener hears, and `outputLatency` is the one that moves under a headphone switch.

`latencyHint` is a request, not a guarantee, and `"interactive"` — the value `createBrowserContext()` passes — is defined as *"Provide the lowest audio output latency possible without glitching. This is the default."* The alternative worth knowing about is `"playback"`: *"Prioritize sustained playback without interruption over audio output latency. Lowest power consumption"* ([`AudioContextLatencyCategory`](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#enumdef-audiocontextlatencycategory)). For a metronome, latency is not a correctness property in the way it is for an instrument — nothing is being triggered by touch and heard immediately — so `"playback"` is defensible on a phone. It is also a *behaviour* change rather than a performance one and would need its own decision; it is not a free win.

Main-thread garbage collection reaches the audio thread only indirectly: it cannot stall the render thread, but it can delay the `setInterval` callback that feeds it, which is exactly the mechanism the look-ahead exists to absorb.

### (b) Scheduler jitter

The HTML standard's timer clamp is mild and does not apply here: *"If timeout is less than 0, then set timeout to 0"* and *"If nesting level is greater than 5, and timeout is less than 4, then set timeout to 4"* ([timer initialisation steps](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/timers-and-user-prompts.html#timer-initialisation-steps)). Polynome asks for 25 ms; nothing clamps that. The same algorithm permits padding: *"Optionally, wait a further implementation-defined length of time. This is intended to allow user agents to pad timeouts as needed to optimize the power usage of the device."*

Background throttling is where the interesting rule lives, and it is engine-specific. Chrome grants **minimal throttling** — no throttling — when the page is visible *or* when *"the page has made noises in the past 30 seconds"*, drops to 1-second timers otherwise, and to 1-minute timers under **intensive throttling** when the page *"has been hidden for more than 5 minutes"*, the chain count is five or greater, *"the page has been silent for at least 30 seconds"* and WebRTC is unused ([Chrome 88 timer throttling](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/timer-throttling-in-chrome-88)). The article is explicit that *"a silent audio track doesn't count"*. Firefox states the exemption differently and more generously: *"Firefox does not throttle inactive tabs if the tab contains an `AudioContext`"*, against a baseline where *"Firefox Desktop has a minimum timeout of 1 second for inactive tabs"* and *"Firefox for Android has a minimum timeout of 15 minutes for inactive tabs and may unload them entirely"* ([MDN, reasons for delays longer than specified](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Window/setTimeout#reasons_for_delays_longer_than_specified)).

The divergence matters for one edge this repository has not considered. Firefox's exemption is conditioned on the *existence* of an `AudioContext`, which Polynome creates on first play and never closes; Chrome's is conditioned on *audible output in the last 30 seconds*. A transport run whose every Step voice is `off`, or whose every layer is muted, is a run that is playing and making no noise — so on Chrome, hidden, it would fall to 1-second timers while the look-ahead is 0.12 s. Nothing would be lost while it stayed silent, but the first ticks after a voice came back would arrive late. **This is derived from the sourced rules and was not verified in a browser.** It is also the strongest argument against ever shortening the look-ahead.

The look-ahead's relationship to jitter is arithmetic: the scheduler survives any stall shorter than `LOOK_AHEAD_SECONDS`, and recovers gracefully from a stall shorter than the horizon minus one tick. At 0.12 s and 25 ms that is 95 ms of tolerance, which the measured 0.017 ms tick and the measured 1.992 ms worst-case reconcile do not come close to consuming.

### (c) Rendering and interaction

The frame budget is not a number the HTML standard mandates, but it does state the model: *"if the browser is attempting to achieve a 60Hz refresh rate, then rendering opportunities occur at a maximum of every 60th of a second (about 16.7ms)"*, and *"if a navigable is not visible, the user agent might decide to drop that page to a much slower 4 rendering opportunities per second, or even less"* ([§ event loop processing model](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/webappapis.html#rendering-opportunity)). A hidden document is removed from the docs to render — the algorithm drops any doc whose *"visibility state is `hidden`"* — so `updateActiveSteps` simply stops re-arming and resumes when the tab returns. That is correct and costs nothing.

The 50 ms figure is the responsiveness threshold, not the frame threshold: *"any of the following occurrences whose duration exceeds 50ms"* is a long task ([W3C Long Tasks](https://w3c.github.io/longtasks/)), restated by the Chrome team as *"any task that takes longer than 50 milliseconds is a long task"* and *"the browser blocks interactions from occurring while a task of any length is running"* ([Optimize long tasks](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-long-tasks)).

`layoutSteps` is already written to the rule the Chrome team gives: *"you should always batch your style reads and do them first (where the browser can use the previous frame's layout values) and then do any writes"* ([Avoid large, complex layouts and layout thrashing](https://web.dev/articles/avoid-large-complex-layouts-and-layout-thrashing)). Per call it performs one `querySelectorAll(".steps")`, then per grid one `querySelector(".beat")`, one `getComputedStyle`, one `clientWidth` read and one `getBoundingClientRect()`, accumulating plans; only then does it write `--beats-per-row` and toggle `is-scrolling`. One forced layout for the whole pass, not one per rhythm — and the function's own comment says so. That ordering is the single most valuable invariant in the file and the easiest thing in the codebase to break by accident.

`updateActiveSteps` is the opposite shape: no layout reads at all, deliberately (its comment explains that restarting the beat animation by reading a box back would cost a whole layout per onset), but a large and unconditional number of selector-engine calls. Per frame, with `C` cycles and `R` rhythms in total, it issues `3C + 2R` `querySelector`/`querySelectorAll` calls unconditionally — three per cycle for the cycle element, its card and its repeat dots, two per rhythm for the layer card and its grid — and `C + R` `CSS.escape` calls, with one further `querySelectorAll(".step")` and one `classList.remove` per control for each rhythm whose drawn step changed that frame. At one cycle and one rhythm that is 5 to 6 selector calls and 2 escapes per frame; at the domain maximum of twelve rhythms in one cycle it is 27 to 39 and 13 — **up to 2,340 selector-engine calls and 780 `CSS.escape` calls per second**, every one of them re-deriving elements that did not move.

### (d) Load and startup

The single-file distribution is 257,726 bytes. [ADR-0009](../adr/0009-adopt-preact-as-the-renderer.md) recorded it growing from 194,762 to 211,773 when Preact was adopted, and called out the 8.7% as a cost worth naming. It has since grown a further 22% with nothing recording it — which is the whole argument for a budget rather than an occasional measurement. About 65 KB of the single file is base64-encoded woff2, so raw bytes there are dominated by fonts; the site build separates the script, which is 151,364 bytes raw.

## 2. Web Audio specifics

### The look-ahead scheduler

The pattern is the Chrome team's and the article is first-party. Its diagnosis of why timers alone fail: *"the actual callback of timer events in JavaScript (through `window.setTimeout()` or `window.setInterval`) can easily be skewed by tens of milliseconds or more by layout, rendering, garbage collection, and XMLHTTPRequest and other callbacks — in short, by any number of things happening on the main execution thread"*. Its recipe: *"A good place to start is probably 100ms of 'lookahead' time, with intervals set to 25ms."* Its statement of the tradeoff: *"The interval between scheduling calls is a tradeoff between the minimum latency and how often your code impacts the processor. How much the lookahead overlaps with the next interval's start time determines how resilient your app will be"* ([A Tale of Two Clocks](https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling)).

Polynome's 25 ms / 0.12 s is the recipe with 20% more horizon. The overlap is 95 ms against the article's 75 ms. **This is already correct and changing either number is a regression risk with no gain.** The only argument for a longer horizon would be Chrome's 1-second background throttle, and that is better answered by not letting a silent-but-playing run reach it than by a horizon a second long, which would make every tempo change take a second to be heard.

### Per-click node allocation

The specification is unusually direct about this being the intended design rather than a compromise. §1.1.6, explaining why the API has no introspection: *"The Web Audio API takes a fire-and-forget approach to audio source scheduling. That is, source nodes are created for each note during the lifetime of the AudioContext, and never explicitly removed from the graph. This is incompatible with a serialization API, since there is no stable set of nodes that could be serialized"* ([§1.1.6](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#lack-of-introspection-or-serialization-primitives)).

§3.1 Dynamic Lifetime, which is non-normative but is the design rationale: *"In a traditional software synthesizer, notes are dynamically allocated and released from a pool of available resources… The audio system automatically deals with tearing-down the part of the routing graph for individual 'note' events… When the note has finished playing, the context will automatically release the reference to the `AudioBufferSourceNode`, which in turn will release references to any nodes it is connected to, and so on. The nodes will automatically get disconnected from the graph and will be deleted when they have no more references. Although it sounds complicated, this all happens automatically with no extra handling required"* ([§3.1](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dynamic-lifetime-background)).

The normative half is the lifetime rule: *"An `AudioScheduledSourceNode` is actively processing if and only if it is playing for at least part of the current rendering quantum"*, and *"`AudioNode`s that are not actively processing output a single channel of silence"* ([§1.5.3](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#AudioNode-actively-processing)). A stopped oscillator stops being actively processing and drops out of the graph.

What the spec calls expensive is the context: *"The subclasses `AudioContext` and `OfflineAudioContext` should be considered expensive objects. Creating these objects may involve creating a high-priority thread, or using a low-latency system audio stream, both having an impact on energy consumption. It is usually not necessary to create more than one `AudioContext` in a document"* ([§1.1.7](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#system-resources-associated-with-baseaudiocontext-subclasses)). Polynome creates one, which is right.

MDN's statement is the closest thing to a direct cost claim from a primary source, and it is about the buffer source rather than the oscillator, so it transfers by analogy rather than by citation: *"An `AudioBufferSourceNode` can only be played once; after each call to `start()`, you have to create a new node if you want to play the same sound again. Fortunately, these nodes are very inexpensive to create, and the actual `AudioBuffer`s can be reused for multiple plays of the sound. Indeed, you can use these nodes in a 'fire and forget' manner: create the node, call `start()` to begin playing the sound, and don't even bother to hold a reference to it"* ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioBufferSourceNode)).

Measured, Polynome allocates **4 nodes per second at the default configuration and 480 per second at the domain maximum running at 300 BPM**. Neither number is one any of these sources suggests is a problem.

**Where the current code is already correct:** one context; per-click oscillator and gain created through the context's own factory methods; `stop()` always called, so the node leaves the graph; an `ended` listener with `{once: true}` that removes the node from `#scheduledSources`, so that set cannot grow without bound.

**Where a change would be theatrical:** node pooling. It would replace a pattern the specification designs for with an object cache that has to reason about `stop()` having been called, cannot reuse an `OscillatorNode` at all (a stopped scheduled source is single-use for the same reason MDN gives for the buffer source), and would save an allocation the spec's own rationale describes as automatic. There is no primary source suggesting node allocation is a bottleneck at these rates, and none was found.

**Where a change would be real but for a different reason:** a pre-rendered `AudioBuffer`. `OfflineAudioContext` is *"a particular type of `BaseAudioContext` for rendering/mixing-down (potentially) faster than real-time"* ([§1.3](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#OfflineAudioContext)), and `e2e/audio-graph.spec.js` already uses it to assert the click envelope frame-accurately. Rendering the three sound profiles × three Step voices once into nine buffers and playing them through `AudioBufferSourceNode` would replace two nodes and three `AudioParam` calls per click with one node and zero. That is a real reduction — but the gain is 720 automation calls per second at the domain maximum against 1,000 that are already pure waste (see below), and the cost is a click that no longer responds to a retuned profile without a rebuild, plus nine buffers to invalidate. **It becomes the right implementation the moment "optional sampled clicks" is built** — AGENTS.md already lists it as a good next addition — and it is not worth doing before then as an optimisation.

### `AudioWorklet`

The Chrome team's rationale for `AudioWorklet` is about *custom DSP* on the render thread: it exists because `ScriptProcessorNode`'s *"event handling is asynchronous by design, and the code execution happens on the main thread. The former induces the latency, and the latter pressures the main thread"*, and `AudioWorklet` *"keeps the user-supplied JavaScript code all within the audio processing thread"* ([Enter Audio Worklet](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/audio-worklet)).

Polynome runs no JavaScript per sample. Its graph is built entirely from native nodes; the main thread's only job is to place `start()`/`stop()` calls and parameter automation ahead of the clock. Moving the *scheduler* into an `AudioWorkletProcessor` is technically possible — the processor is called once per render quantum and could compute event times itself — and would immunise the scheduler against main-thread stalls entirely. It would also require the pattern, the tempo curve and every Configuration edit to cross a `MessagePort`, put the timing maths somewhere no `node --test` file can reach it, and buy margin against a stall that has been measured at 1.992 ms in a 95 ms window. **Not warranted.** It would be warranted if a future feature needed per-sample control the node graph cannot express, and nothing on the AGENTS.md "good next additions" list does.

### `setTargetAtTime` versus direct assignment, and the traffic nobody counted

`setTargetAtTime` *"start[s] exponentially approaching the target value at the given time with a rate having the given time constant"*, with *"v(t) = V₁ + (V₀ − V₁)·e^(−(t−T₀)/τ)"*, and the spec notes *"the parameter value does not immediately change to the target value at the given time, but instead gradually changes to the target value"* ([§1.6.2](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dom-audioparam-settargetattime)). `#syncNodes()` uses τ = 0.01, so a mix change is 63% of the way there in 10 ms and inaudibly short of the target after 50 ms. That is exactly right for a level or balance drag, and it is why the code uses it rather than assigning `.value`: an assignment would step and click.

The cost is not the maths. It is that every automation call is a message from the control thread to the rendering thread: *"Communication from the control thread to the rendering thread is done using control message passing"*, and *"each `AudioContext` has a single control message queue"* ([§2.2](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#control-message-queue)). The spec does not price a control message and no primary source found does, so the claim here is about the *count*, which is a property of this code and not of any engine:

| Configuration | Clicks/s | Oscillators + gains/s | `connect()`/s | AudioParam calls/s | of which `setTargetAtTime` | Share that is mix re-assertion |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 × 4/4, subdivision 1, 120 BPM | 2 | 4 | 4 | 126.0 | 120 | **95%** |
| 12 × 8/4, subdivision 4, 300 BPM | 240 | 480 | 480 | 1,720.0 | 1,000 | **58%** |

`#schedule()` calls `#syncNodes()` unconditionally on every tick. `#syncNodes()` issues one `setTargetAtTime` for the master gain plus two per layer — gain and pan — so forty ticks per second cost `40 × (1 + 2L)`. At one layer that is 120 calls per second, against six that a click actually needs. The function's own comment says *"Syncing the graph is the engine's own overhead and it costs render time, which on a phone waking from an interruption is not a rounding error"* — the code already knows this is a cost, and pays it forty times a second anyway.

The reason it is there is an invariant: the graph is never more than one tick behind `#state`. But `start()` calls `#syncNodes()`, `updateMix()` calls `#syncNodes()`, and every edit that adds or removes a layer carries the `restart-transport-run` consequence, which routes through `start()`. The per-tick call appears to guarantee nothing the narrow paths do not already guarantee. **Establishing that explicitly — a dirty flag set by the three paths that change the graph, cleared by the sync — is the single highest-value change identified here**, and it is a behavioural change to the engine's contract, so it wants an ADR rather than a commit.

### `suspend()` while stopped

The spec offers the invitation: *"`suspend` and `close` allow authors to release system resources, including threads, processes and audio streams. Suspending a `BaseAudioContext` permits implementations to release some of its resources"* ([§1.1.7](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#system-resources-associated-with-baseaudiocontext-subclasses)). A stopped Polynome holds a live audio callback thread for the life of the page and only silences it by zeroing the master gain.

**Do not take the invitation.** `docs/research/ios-safari-web-audio.md` establishes that a suspended context on iOS has to be *allowed to start* again, that WebKit parks the promise `resume()` returns and settles it neither way when it is not, and that this is the single highest-value cause of silent, error-free failure. [ADR-0005](../adr/0005-own-the-audio-context-lifecycle.md) is built on never awaiting that promise. Suspending on every stop would put the application through that gate on every play, in exchange for releasing a thread on a page the user is looking at and about to press play on again. The power saving is real and unquantified; the failure mode is documented and severe.

## 3. Measurement — how you actually know

### Clocks

`performance.now()` is coarsened for security: MDN gives *"Resolution in isolated contexts: 5 microseconds"* and *"Resolution in non-isolated contexts: 100 microseconds"*, *"to offer protection against timing attacks and fingerprinting"* ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Performance/now)). Polynome is served without COOP/COEP and is not cross-origin isolated, so in-page timings are quantised to 0.1 ms. That is coarser than the entire measured scheduler tick. **Any in-page attempt to time `plan()` or one scheduler tick directly would be measuring the clock, not the code** — a decisive argument for counting work in the browser and timing only in Node, where `performance.now()` *"returns the current high resolution millisecond timestamp"* with no such clamp ([Node `perf_hooks`](https://nodejs.org/api/perf_hooks.html)).

`AudioContext.getOutputTimestamp()` is the bridge between the two clocks: it returns *"the `contextTime` member … the time of the sample frame which is currently being rendered by the audio output device"* and *"the `performanceTime` member … the time estimating the moment when the sample frame corresponding to the stored `contextTime` value was rendered by the audio output device, in the same units and origin as `performance.now()`"*, and *"after the context's rendering graph has started processing of blocks of audio, its `currentTime` attribute value always exceeds the `contextTime` value obtained from `getOutputTimestamp`"* ([§1.2.3](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dom-audiocontext-getoutputtimestamp)). It is the only specified way to answer "how far ahead of the speaker is the clock I am scheduling against", and it is the right instrument for aligning the visual playhead if that ever matters.

### `PerformanceObserver`

MDN documents `element`, `event`, `first-input`, `largest-contentful-paint`, `layout-shift`, `long-animation-frame`, `longtask`, `mark`, `measure`, `navigation`, `paint`, `resource`, `taskattribution` and `visibility-state` as entry types ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/PerformanceEntry/entryType)), with `longtask` reporting *"instances of long tasks"* and `layout-shift` reporting *"layout stability of web pages based on movements of the elements on the page"*. One API detail matters for writing the observer: *"Whenever you need to configure the observer to use the `buffered` or `durationThreshold` option, use `type` instead of `entryType`"* ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/PerformanceObserver/observe)).

For Polynome, `longtask` is the only one worth observing, and it is worth observing as a **boolean**: during playback, are there any tasks over 50 ms? A zero-or-one assertion at a threshold 3,000× the measured tick is robust in a way a millisecond figure never is. `layout-shift` and `element` are load-time metrics for content sites and have no bearing here. `event` timing would matter if interaction latency were ever in question; it is not, and adding it would be instrumentation for a problem nobody has reported.

| `AudioContext.currentTime` progress | Specified ([§1.1.1](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dom-baseaudiocontext-currenttime)) | Liveness — already how ADR-0017 detects a stalled clock |
| DevTools **WebAudio** panel: State, Sample Rate, Callback Buffer Size, Max Output channels, Current Time, **Callback Interval**, **Render Capacity** | Chrome-only ([Chrome DevTools](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/webaudio)) | Render Capacity is *"a percentage showing how the web audio renderer performs against its render budget"* — the only direct read on audio-thread headroom, and it exists in no other engine |
| CDP `Performance.getMetrics` | Chrome-only, stable domain ([DevTools Protocol](https://chromedevtools.github.io/devtools-protocol/tot/Performance/)) | Counters, not audio |

The asymmetry is worth stating plainly: **Render Capacity is the single most useful audio-performance number available in any browser, and it exists only in Chrome DevTools, only interactively, and is not exposed to page script or to CDP in any documented form.** Anything that wants it has to be a human looking at a panel. There is no automated substitute.

On Chromium command-line switches: the historical `--enable-benchmarking` does **not** exist under that name in current Chromium. Reading the source, `components/variations/variations_switches.h` declares `kEnableBenchmarkingApi` (`--enable-benchmarking-api`) and `chrome/common/chrome_switches.cc` declares `kEnableNetBenchmarking` (`--enable-net-benchmarking`, *"Enables the network-related benchmarking extensions"*). Neither is a general "make timings reproducible" switch, and no Chrome-team documentation was found describing one. Treat any advice to pass `--enable-benchmarking` as stale.

### Playwright's CDP access

`await browserContext.newCDPSession(page)` returns a `CDPSession` with `send`, `on` and `detach`, and the documentation states flatly that *"CDP sessions are only supported on Chromium-based browsers"* ([BrowserContext](https://playwright.dev/docs/api/class-browsercontext), [CDPSession](https://playwright.dev/docs/api/class-cdpsession)). The `Performance` domain is stable and offers `Performance.enable` with a `timeDomain` of `timeTicks` or `threadTicks`, `Performance.getMetrics` returning *"current values for run-time metrics"* as `{name, value}` pairs, and `Performance.disable` ([DevTools Protocol](https://chromedevtools.github.io/devtools-protocol/tot/Performance/)).

Polynome's Playwright project is Chromium-only, so this is available. What it is good for is **counters, not durations**: metrics such as node and document counts, layout counts and recalc-style counts are integers describing work done, and they are far more stable across runners than any of the `*Duration` metrics in the same set, which are thread CPU time and vary with the machine. `browserContext.tracing` is Playwright's own debugging trace, produced as a zip for the trace viewer; it is not a Chrome performance trace and is not an assertion surface. Assertions against a Playwright trace's contents are possible but undocumented as a stable API, and would be a poor foundation.

## 4. Performance regression testing that survives CI

GitHub's standard `ubuntu-latest` runner is **4 vCPUs and 16 GB for public repositories, 2 vCPUs and 8 GB for private**, and *"with the exception of single-CPU runners, each GitHub-hosted runner is a new virtual machine (VM) hosted by GitHub"*, with Linux runners *"hosted in Azure"* ([GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/runners/github-hosted-runners)). A fresh VM per job removes one source of drift — no warm caches from the last run — and leaves the two that matter: a shared hypervisor and a neighbour.

### What can be asserted deterministically

Four categories, all of them exact, all of them reachable from what the repository already has.

**Allocated audio work, through the existing seam.** `MetronomeEngine` already takes `options.createContext`, and `test/metronome-audio.test.js` already has a hand-written `FakeAudioContext` that records every node and every automation call. Counting is a strictly smaller ask than what that double already does. A run of N simulated ticks yields exact integers: oscillators created, gains created, `connect()` calls, `start()`/`stop()` pairs, and automation calls broken down by method. These are properties of the code, not the machine, and they are identical on every runner.

**Planned-event structure.** `plan()` is a pure function of the transport's state and the two times it is given. Over 200 identical runs of the 400-tick worst-case workload it returned **exactly one distinct event count**. The assertions worth making are the invariants rather than the number: every absolute step emitted exactly once across a long run, `audioTime` non-decreasing, every event inside `[currentTime − 4 ms, horizon)`, and no event for an `off` position. A raw count as a supporting ratchet catches a loop bound quietly changing.

**DOM operation counts, through instrumented stubs.** In an `addInitScript`, wrap `Element.prototype.querySelector`, `Element.prototype.querySelectorAll`, `Element.prototype.getBoundingClientRect`, `window.getComputedStyle` and `CSSStyleDeclaration.prototype.setProperty` with counters, run a fixed number of animation frames or a single `renderCycles()`, and assert counts. Two assertions matter and they are different in kind. The first is a budget: selector-engine calls per frame. The second is an **ordering invariant** — during a `layoutSteps()` pass, no layout-forcing read may occur after the first style write. That one is not a threshold at all; it is a correctness property, it is exactly the rule the Chrome team states, and it is the property `layoutSteps` was written to hold and has no test for.

**Artifact bytes.** `scripts/build.ts` is byte-deterministic: three consecutive `buildDistribution({target: "single-file"})` runs produced identical SHA-256 digests and identical lengths, in 17–150 ms. `test/bundle.test.ts` and `test/site.test.ts` already call it. A raw-byte budget costs nothing and would have noticed the 22% growth since ADR-0009.

One caveat on compressed budgets, measured: `zlib.gzipSync(buffer, {level: 9})` in Node and the system `gzip -9` disagree by 99 bytes on identical input (37,035 against 36,936 for the `c2d1914` `site/app-local.js`), and brotli disagrees too (31,809 against 31,866). **Raw bytes are the only fully portable artifact number.** A gzip budget is still worth having, because raw bytes for the single-file distribution are dominated by base64 fonts and a compressed figure tracks code more honestly — but it has to be computed by one named compressor at one named level and given more headroom than a raw budget needs.

### What has to be a statistic, and why the usual mitigations under-deliver

Timing anything requires a distribution and a threshold, and the threshold has to survive a neighbour. Measured on an **idle laptop**, which is the best case a CI runner will never have:

| Measure | min | median | p95 | max | CV | max/min |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Absolute ms, 400 ticks of worst-case planning | 2.88 | 3.009 | 4.029 | 4.242 | 8.7% | 1.47× |
| Same, as a ratio to a control workload run in the same process | 2.794 | 3.413 | 3.813 | 4.714 | 8.2% | 1.69× |
| Planned event count, 200 runs | 1128 | 1128 | 1128 | 1128 | 0% | 1.00× |

The middle row is the finding worth dwelling on. Normalising against a control workload run in the same process is the standard advice for making a timing comparable across machines, and **on this sample it did not reduce variance at all** — 8.2% against 8.7%, with a *wider* max/min spread. The reason is that a same-run control corrects for a *sustained* difference in machine speed, which is the thing that differs between a laptop and an Azure VM; it cannot correct for GC pauses, JIT tier transitions and scheduler preemption inside a single measurement window, which is what the variance here actually is. The technique is worth using when comparing two machines and is not a substitute for warmup and medians when comparing two runs on one.

Heap-delta measurement is worse. Sampling `process.memoryUsage().heapUsed` around one simulated minute of work, with a forced `gc()` before, across four runs:

| Run | Flat envelope | Peak envelope |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 954 KiB | 125 KiB |
| 2 | 914 KiB | 637 KiB |
| 3 | 631 KiB | 787 KiB |
| 4 | 481 KiB | 468 KiB |

The Peak envelope allocates strictly more than the Flat one — it is the same code plus two to four frozen objects per curve evaluation — and this measurement reports the opposite in two runs out of four, because a collection landing inside one window and not the other dominates the signal entirely. **Assert allocation counts through an instrumented seam; never assert heap bytes.**

The Chrome team's own guidance on the same problem, from the Lighthouse variability documentation, names seven sources of variance including *"client resource contention"* — *"other applications running on the same machine while Lighthouse is running can cause contention for CPU, memory, and network resources"* — and *"browser nondeterminism"*. Its mitigations are the standard ones: *"use aggregate values like the median, 90th percentile, or even min/max instead of single test results"*, *"the median Lighthouse score of 5 runs is twice as stable as 1 run"*, and, decisively for this question: *"In continuous integration situations, use dedicated servers when possible. Free CI environments and 'burstable' instances are typically quite volatile"* ([Lighthouse variability](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/docs/variability.md)).

Node's own benchmarking guide reaches for statistics rather than thresholds. Its `compare.js` runs each benchmark 30 times by default and *"performs an independent/unpaired 2-group t-test, with the null hypothesis that the performance is the same for both versions"*, and warns *"if there are no stars, then don't make any conclusions based on the improvement"* ([writing and running benchmarks](https://github.com/nodejs/node/blob/main/doc/contributing/writing-and-running-benchmarks.md)). That is the right shape for comparing two commits interactively. It is the wrong shape for a CI gate, because it needs both versions in the same run and it answers "did this change things" rather than "is this acceptable".

The dependency argument is AGENTS.md's own: *"weigh a new tool by the regressions it would catch, and do not expect a haul"*. Every timing tool above catches the class of regression that this document's measurements say does not exist here, and none of them catches the class that does — an extra 900 automation calls per second, or a selector call moved inside a loop.

### Why time-based assertions on CI runners flake, stated once

A shared VM gives a job a slice of a host it does not control. The variance that produces is not noise around a stable mean; it is a mixture of a fast regime and a preempted one, so the distribution has a tail and no useful standard deviation. Every mitigation is a way of reducing the weight of that tail, and none removes it: warmup discards JIT tiering but not preemption; median-of-N reduces the chance that the reported figure came from the tail but does not bound the tail; a same-run control corrects for sustained speed differences between machines but — measured above — not for short-timescale noise on one; and a ratchet against a committed baseline turns a threshold into a comparison across two different machines on two different days, which is the worst version of the problem, not a fix for it.

The conclusion is not "measure more carefully". It is that **a threshold on time can only be set so loosely that it catches nothing, or so tightly that it fails on a busy Tuesday** — and between those, for this application, there is no useful band, because the quantity being measured is 0.017 ms and the noise floor is milliseconds. Count the work instead. Work counts are exact, they are the thing that actually changed when a regression lands, and they read as an assertion about the code rather than about the runner.

### Not worth doing

- **`AudioWorklet` for the scheduler.** Buys immunity to a main-thread stall measured at 1.992 ms inside a 95 ms window, at the cost of moving the timing maths out of `node --test`'s reach.
- **Node pooling.** Contradicts the specification's stated design ([§1.1.6](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#lack-of-introspection-or-serialization-primitives), [§3.1](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#dynamic-lifetime-background)) and cannot reuse a stopped source node anyway.
- **Pre-rendered `AudioBuffer` clicks, as an optimisation.** Right implementation for sampled clicks when that feature is built; wrong reason to build it now.
- **`AudioContext.suspend()` while stopped.** Trades a thread nobody is short of for the failure mode `ios-safari-web-audio.md` and ADR-0005 exist to avoid.
- **Anything inside `plan()` for speed.** 0.00721 ms per tick at the domain maximum. A 10× improvement would be invisible.
- **Replacing `localeCompare` in the event sort.** It runs only on an `audioTime` tie, over a list averaging 2.82 events per tick at the maximum.
- **Shortening the look-ahead.** The 95 ms margin is the thing absorbing every stall measured here and the thing standing between the application and Chrome's 1-second background throttle.
- **A gzip budget as the *only* artifact budget.** Node's zlib and the system gzip disagree by 99 bytes on identical input.

## Method, and what it does not cover

Everything numeric attributed to this repository was measured against the working tree at commit `c2d1914` on an Apple M2 running macOS 25.4 and Node 22.23.1, using throwaway scripts outside the repository. That tree acquired uncommitted ADR-0017 recovery work in `metronome.js` part-way through; it touches neither `#schedule()`, `#syncNodes()` nor `#scheduleClick()`, and every behavioural count was re-taken against it and came out identical. `model.js` and `shared-transport.js` were copied and the copy of `model.js` instrumented with call counters; the engine measurements imported the repository's real `metronome.js` and drove it through `options.createContext` with a counting double and a stubbed `window.setInterval`, so the code under measurement is the shipped code. Timings are the median of eleven or fifteen repetitions after warmup; call counts are exact and were taken in a separate uninstrumented pass.

The one class of figure that uncommitted work does move is artifact bytes, since it is 3 KB of new source. The sizes quoted in this document — and the ratchets in the suite above — are taken against the working tree **including** it: 257,726 raw for `dist/polynome.html` and 151,364 raw / 37,642 gzip-9 for `site/app-local.js`, against 254,588 and 148,362 / 37,035 at `c2d1914`. `site/styles-local.css` is 26,490 either way, which is the cross-check that only the script moved. Whoever lands the artifact-size test should re-take all four numbers rather than trusting these, because ADR-0017 will have moved them again.

Two configurations are used throughout. The **default** is one 4/4 rhythm layer at subdivision one and 120 BPM. The **domain maximum** is twelve rhythm layers at 8/4 and subdivision four in one Cycle — 384 pattern positions, the same worst case ADR-0009 measured the renderer against. The engine measurements run the domain maximum at 300 BPM, the top of `TEMPO_LIMIT`, to reach the highest click rate the domain permits; the `plan()` measurements run it at 120 BPM. Peak is the expensive envelope shape and Flat the cheap one, and both are shown wherever they differ.

What this does not cover, stated so it is not mistaken for covered:

- **No browser was driven.** Every DOM figure here — selector-engine calls per frame, the read/write ordering in `layoutSteps` — is counted from the source, not measured in Chromium. The cost of a selector call, a `classList.toggle` and a style recalculation on a real engine is unknown to this document.
- **No real `AudioContext` was exercised.** Node has none. The per-click node allocation cost, the real cost of an `AudioParam` control message, and the render-thread load are all unmeasured; the argument about them rests on the specification and MDN, and on counting the calls this code makes.
- **The Preact figures are ADR-0009's**, taken before the ADR-0008 rebase and not re-run here.
- **No measurement was taken on a GitHub Actions runner.** The variance figures are from an idle laptop and are therefore a floor, not an estimate.
- **The Chrome background-throttling edge** — a playing but entirely silent transport run falling to 1-second timers in a hidden tab — is derived from the sourced rule and was not reproduced.
- **`--enable-benchmarking`** could not be found in current Chromium under that name; `--enable-benchmarking-api` and `--enable-net-benchmarking` exist and do something else. No Chrome-team documentation of a reproducible-timing switch was located.
- **Render Capacity** is described from Chrome's DevTools documentation; it was not read off a running context, and no way to reach it from script or CDP was found.
